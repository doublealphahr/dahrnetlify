The official name of South Korea's "Yellow Envelope Act" is the Amendment to the Trade Union and Labor Relations Adjustment Act, which officially took effect on March 10, 2026. Its core English content revolves around two major provisions: expanding the definition of "employer" to penetrate outsourcing and contractual structures, and restricting corporations from claiming damages against lawful strikes.

 

 

Core Provision 1: Expanded Definition of "Employer" (Article 2)

The amendment expands the definition of "Employer" from the traditional "party that signs the employment contract" to "a person who has substantial and concrete control or decision-making power over working conditions", even if that entity is not the direct contractual counterparty of the worker.

"An employer is defined as a person who can substantially and concretely control and determine working conditions, even if not a party to the labor contract."

The direct consequence of this change is that in structures where the "legal employer and the actual user of labor are separated"—such as principal-subcontractor arrangements, dispatch employment, and EOR (Employer of Record) models—the party actually exercising control may be recognized as the employer, thereby bearing the statutory obligation to engage in collective bargaining with outsourced workers.

Core Provision 2: Restrictions on Strike Damages (Article 3)

The amendment strictly limits the ability of corporations to file massive civil damages lawsuits against union members participating in lawful strikes. Previously, companies often used exorbitant damage claims as a tool to dismantle strikes; the new law substantially weakens this bargaining tool.

In addition, the amendment expands the scope of matters that can trigger lawful labor disputes to include management decisions affecting working conditions (such as layoffs and outsourcing decisions), rather than being limited to narrow labor conditions such as wages and working hours.
