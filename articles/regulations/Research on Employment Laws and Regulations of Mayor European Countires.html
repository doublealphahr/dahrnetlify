A Comparative Overview of Labor Law Frameworks in France, Germany, Switzerland, the Netherlands, and China

 

Authors: Chen Cheng, Partner, Huiye Law Firm

Urs Marti, Special Foreign Labor Law Expert, Huiye Labor and Human Resources Professional Committee

Published: 2019-03-01

In today's global economy, Chinese companies are becoming increasingly internationalized. Since most countries have many mandatory legal provisions in labor law, Chinese companies hiring overseas need immediate legal advice and solutions with an international perspective, in order to anticipate risks, make reasonable arrangements, and protect the company's lawful rights and interests in different jurisdictions.

This article focuses on four major European countries — France, Germany, Switzerland, and the Netherlands — and at the same time refers to China's labor law framework, providing a comparative overview through concise summary descriptions in the following aspects:

  • Employee hiring, remuneration, and termination methods;

  • Rights of employers and employees at and after hiring;

  • Protection of confidential information and commercial interests;

  • Employee consultation, trade unions, and chambers of commerce;

  • Employment-related costs.

1. Overview of National Labor Laws

(1) France

  • French labor law provides a high standard of protection for workers.

  • With government reforms and the evolution of case law, the legal environment is also changing.

  • Union representatives have extensive rights. Most notably, they have the right to nominate candidates in the first round of voting for employee representative bodies.

  • Wrong choices can lead to costly individual or collective litigation.

(2) Germany

  • German labor law provides a high standard of protection for workers against dismissal. Therefore, in Germany, it is quite common for workers to challenge their dismissal in court and reach a settlement on severance pay.

  • Employment conditions and the nature of labor relations are usually determined by collective bargaining agreements between trade unions and employer associations/employers, or by works agreements (agreements between the works council and the employer).

  • Employee participation is very high, and the law grants works councils the right to participate in co-determination (an important factor in employee hiring and dismissal, health and safety matters, human resource planning, and restructuring).

(3) Switzerland

  • Compared with other countries, Swiss labor law is more favorable to employers.

  • Labor relations in Switzerland are stable, and strikes are rare.

  • In principle, Swiss employers may terminate the employment relationship for any reason (at-will dismissal), and dismissed employees are only entitled to request that the employer provide the written reason for dismissal.

  • Signing a termination agreement is a feasible approach, including during the employee's statutory protected period (for example, during illness and pregnancy).

(4) The Netherlands

  • In 2015, a large number of legal provisions were enacted, making it more difficult for employers to dismiss employees, which in practice led to greater use of termination agreements.

  • Employees who have worked for at least 2 years are entitled to compensation upon termination of the employment relationship, amounting to approximately one-third of a month's salary, accumulated by years of service.

  • In most cases, employers cannot terminate the employment relationship without obtaining permission from the employee insurance agency or resorting to the district court.

  • New rules have begun to apply to fixed-term labor contracts, including the use of non-compete clauses and the payment of one month's salary compensation upon termination (unless one month's notice is given).

(5) China

  • Chinese labor law provides a high standard of protection for workers against dismissal. Therefore, in China, it is quite common for workers to refuse to accept their dismissal in court and demand restoration of the employment relationship or claim high compensation.

  • Chinese labor law to some extent leads to excessively high employment costs for enterprises. For example, employers are obliged to sign an indefinite-term labor contract with a worker after signing two fixed-term labor contracts, or after the worker has worked for the employer for ten years (the employer's right to terminate is strictly limited); employers must also pay compensation to workers when the labor contract expires and is not renewed, and so on.

  • China has numerous local regulations and departmental documents, which to a considerable extent increases employers' labor compliance pressure.

  • China's current labor relations are generally stable, and strikes are rare.

2. EU Level

The specific operation of EU labor law in each member state often differs (because most EU employment regulations are incorporated into each member state's own national law). Areas covered by EU labor law include:

  1. Working hours, part-time work, and fixed-term work.

  2. Anti-discrimination, protection of pregnant employees, and equal pay for equal work.

  3. Notification and consultation of employees on employment-related issues, including collective redundancies and business transfers.

  4. Protection of employee rights in the event of business transfers.

It should be noted that among the four European countries covered in this article, Switzerland is not an EU member state.

3. National Level

Each country has established complex laws and systems aimed at protecting the interests of workers and helping ensure a minimum standard of living for all its people. The legal framework usually includes three legal systems:

  1. Labor law governs individual employment contracts.

  2. Collective/industrial relations law regulates the negotiation, adoption, and enforcement of collective agreements, the organizational activities of trade unions, and industrial action by employers and employees.

  3. Social security law provides social responses to needs and conditions that have a significant impact on residents' quality of life, such as aging, disability, death, illness, and unemployment.

4. Specific Comparison

Comparison 1 — Paid Leave

Country Key Rules
France

  • At least 25 days of paid annual leave per year.

  • In addition, for most employees, approximately 11 days of paid public holidays per year (equivalent to statutory holidays).

  • Collective bargaining agreements (CBAs) often grant additional paid leave (e.g., seniority-based paid leave).
Germany

  • Statutory minimum 20 days of paid annual leave per year (for employees working five days a week).

  • Depending on employee seniority and industry, many employees enjoy 25 to 30 days of paid annual leave per year.
Switzerland (non-EU)

  • Minimum statutory paid annual leave is 20 days per year (employees under 20 years old may enjoy 25 days of paid annual leave).

  • 25 days of paid annual leave per year is common among employees over 50 years old.
Netherlands

  • Full-time employees enjoy at least 20 days of statutory paid annual leave per year.

  • Collective labor agreements or individual employment agreements usually grant employees longer annual leave.

  • In addition, there are 9 public holidays per year.
China

  • Minimum statutory paid annual leave is 5 days per year (for employees who have worked continuously for 12 months or more).

  • For employees with 1 to 10 years of cumulative service, statutory paid annual leave is 5 days; 10 to 20 years, 10 days; 20 years or more, 15 days.

  • Employees of foreign-invested enterprises often enjoy more paid annual leave than the statutory standard.

Comparison 2 — Maternity Leave / Family Leave

Country Key Rules
France

  • 16 weeks of maternity leave; for a third child, maternity leave is extended to 26 months.

  • Women on maternity leave receive social security payments up to 81% of their normal salary. Many collective bargaining agreements require employers to top this up to full normal salary.

  • 3 days of fully paid paternity leave.
Germany

  • Maternity leave is 6 weeks before the expected due date.

  • 8 weeks after childbirth (may be 12 weeks), during which the employer must pay full net salary. Employers may choose to pay more.

  • Either parent may take up to 3 years of unpaid parental leave.
Switzerland

  • Maternity leave is 14 weeks after childbirth (but female employees may start earlier).

  • Daily health insurance fund pays 80% of salary or a maximum of CHF 196 per day.

  • There is no statutory paternity leave.
Netherlands

  • Maternity leave is 16 weeks, and the start time should be no later than 4 weeks before the expected due date.

  • Social insurance pays 100% of the female employee's pre-maternity salary, but there is a cap of EUR 214.28 per day.
China

  • Maternity leave is 98 days, of which 15 days may be taken before the expected due date; for difficult births, an additional 15 days of maternity leave; for multiple births, an additional 15 days per additional baby.

  • On the basis of the above provisions, provinces and municipalities have generally increased maternity leave days, with most provinces and cities adding 30 to 60 days.

  • The maternity insurance fund pays maternity leave wages according to the employer's average monthly wage for employees in the previous year. Many local regulations require employers to top up the difference if individual wages are higher than the average wage.

  • There is no unified national paternity leave. However, most provinces and cities have clear fully paid paternity leave provisions, ranging from 7 to 30 days.

Comparison 3 — Trade Unions / Labor Strikes

Country Key Rules
France

  • "Representative" unions (those considered to represent a group of employees) and elected employee representative bodies (works committees, health and safety committees, groups) have extensive powers.

  • Strikes are very common, not only in the public sector.
Germany

  • The main role of trade unions in Germany is to promote collective labor agreements.

  • The role of union representatives is to support employees and the works council, but they have no right to participate in company affairs.

  • In recent years, strikes in Germany have become increasingly common.
Switzerland

  • The main role of trade unions in Switzerland is to promote collective labor agreements.

  • Due to the unique concept of "social partnership," Swiss labor relations have remained relatively stable over the past few decades.

  • Short-term strikes are rare in Switzerland, and long-term strikes almost never occur.
Netherlands

  • About 20% of the labor force has joined a union, but this proportion is declining.

  • Unions in manufacturing and semi-public or privatized industry are well organized, but in service and emerging technology industries, unions are less organized.

  • Strikes are uncommon.
China

  • The main role of trade unions in China is to coordinate labor relations through equal consultation and collective contract systems, and to safeguard employees' labor rights and interests.

  • When an employer unilaterally dismisses an employee, it should notify the trade union.

  • Short-term strikes are rare in China, and long-term strikes almost never occur.

Comparison 4 — Employer's Notice of Dismissal / Severance Pay

Country Key Rules
France

  • Dismissal requires a real and serious reason, and must also comply with special/formal procedures.

  • For employees with less than 2 years of service, the notice period is 1 month; for employees with more than 2 years of service, the notice period is 2 months (3 months for managers).

  • Severance pay is calculated based on the employee's years of service, with one-quarter of a month's salary payable for each year of service; for employees with more than 10 years of service, one-third of a month's salary is payable for each year of service exceeding 10 years.
Germany

  • The employer's right to dismiss is strictly restricted by German law.

  • For employees with less than 2 years of service, the notice period is 4 weeks; for employees with 2 to 20 years of service, the notice period ranges from 1 month to 7 months (increased proportionally).

  • If no special circumstances exist, severance pay is 18 months' salary.

  • Termination agreements are common in Germany.
Switzerland

  • In Switzerland, employers do not need a reason to dismiss an employee.

  • The notice period for dismissal in the first year is 1 month; the notice period for dismissal in years 2 to 9 is 2 months; the notice period for dismissal after 10 years of service is 3 months.

  • In rare cases, compensation of 2 to 3 months' salary (up to 6 months' salary) applies.

  • Signing a termination agreement is a feasible approach, including when the employee is in a statutory protected period (illness, pregnancy, etc.).
Netherlands

  • Dismissal requires a "fair reason."

  • For employees with less than 5 years of service, the notice period is 1 month; for employees with 5 to 15 years of service, the notice period is increased by 1 month for every 5 years of service; for employees with more than 15 years of service, the notice period is 4 months.

  • For employees with 2 or more years of continuous service, the employer must pay severance pay (i.e., "transition payment"). For each year of service, one-third of a month's salary is payable; for employees with more than 10 years of service, one-half of a month's salary is payable for each year exceeding 10 years. The severance cap is EUR 81,000 (2019 rule) or the employee's total annual salary (whichever is higher).
China

  • The employer's right to dismiss is strictly restricted by Chinese law.

  • In most cases, the employer must give 1 month's notice, and that notice period may be replaced by paying 1 month's salary.

  • Except in cases involving serious employee fault, the employer must pay compensation. Compensation is calculated based on the employee's years of service, with 1 month's salary payable for each year of service, but subject to caps on amount and years.

  • Signing a termination agreement is a feasible approach.

Comparison 5 — Non-Compete Clauses

Country Key Rules
France

  • Non-compete clauses can still be enforced after termination of the employment relationship.

  • The duration and geographical scope of the non-compete clause should be commensurate with business needs, and the clause must not prevent the employee from working.

  • The enforceability of the non-compete clause is premised on the employer paying corresponding compensation to the employee.
Germany

  • Non-compete clauses can still be enforced after termination of the employment relationship.

  • The duration and geographical scope of the non-compete clause should be commensurate with business needs (at most 2 years), and restrictions should be relaxed for managers/board members.

  • The enforceability of the non-compete clause is premised on the employer paying 50% of the employee's salary during the non-compete period.
Switzerland

  • Non-compete clauses can still be enforced after termination of the employment relationship.

  • The duration and geographical scope of the non-compete clause should be commensurate with business needs, and the clause must not prevent the employee from carrying out their work.

  • No compensation needs to be paid to the employee.
Netherlands

  • Non-compete clauses can still be enforced after termination of the employment relationship (there are exceptions in fixed-term labor contracts).

  • The duration, geographical scope, and ambit of the non-compete clause should be reasonable, and the relevant court has the right to modify or revoke it.

  • No compensation needs to be paid to the employee, but if the employee is severely restricted in finding alternative work, the court may make different rulings on the compensation issue.
China

  • Non-compete clauses can still be enforced after termination of the employment relationship, but the duration must not exceed 2 years.

  • The persons subject to non-compete restrictions are limited to senior management, senior technical personnel, and other employees with confidentiality obligations; the scope of the non-compete restriction and the geographical scope must be lawful and reasonable.

  • The enforceability of the non-compete clause is premised on the employer paying corresponding compensation to the employee. In the absence of an agreement between the employer and the employee, the court will support the employee's claim that "non-compete compensation shall not be lower than 30% of the average monthly wage for the 12 months prior to termination of the employment relationship." If the employer terminates the non-compete agreement during the non-compete period, the employee may be requested to pay an additional 3 months of economic compensation.

5. Conclusion

In view of the above comparison, the following points should be noted when considering hiring overseas employees:

  1. Leave-related laws (for example: vacation, sick leave, and maternity leave).

  2. The power of trade unions (unions may bear a lot of responsibility, and employee benefits and working conditions may be strictly controlled by collective labor agreements).

  3. Termination of the employment relationship (notice period, reason for dismissal, severance pay).

  4. Non-compete agreements (the host country may prohibit non-compete agreements).

  5. Employee privacy (the host country may have strict laws governing and restricting the circulation of employee information).
