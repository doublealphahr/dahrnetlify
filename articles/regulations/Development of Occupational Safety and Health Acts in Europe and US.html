Industrial Revolution and the Emergence of OSH Legislation, the 1970s Legislative Wave, and the Reference Value of US OSH Law

 

On December 1, 2014, China's newly amended Work Safety Law officially came into effect, comprehensively regulating and guiding national work safety for the coming period, protecting workers' lives, safety, and physical and mental health, and safeguarding people's well-being and social harmony. Looking around the world, occupational safety and health legislation originated and was standardized in developed countries in Europe and the US. These developed countries, which pioneered the Industrial Revolution and entered industrialized society earlier, have set a model for the world in building occupational safety and health regulations.

Industrial Revolution and the Emergence of OSH Legislation

Occupational safety and health regulations evolved from nothing to something, from single, sporadic regulations applicable only to a specific scope (industry, region, worker) to comprehensive, all-encompassing basic laws with broader application, supplemented by a series of subordinate regulations and standards, forming a relatively complete occupational safety and health regulatory system. This process went through a considerable historical stage. It is generally believed that occupational safety and health legislation emerged during the First Industrial Revolution in Europe, arising along with the industrialization and urbanization of Europe and the US.

The Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the 1760s. It was a production and technological revolution that replaced human power with machines and replaced individual workshop manual production with large-scale factory production. The Industrial Revolution achieved an important transformation from a traditional agricultural society to a modern industrial society, but also brought increasingly prominent occupational safety and health hazards. As the Industrial Revolution deepened, production scale expanded rapidly, and industrial accidents such as mine disasters, fires, explosions, and machinery injuries occurred continuously, prompting people to control increasingly serious industrial accidents from a legal perspective.

During the British Industrial Revolution, employees' working hours, wages, and other working conditions were determined by the "contract" signed between employer and employee. National law only protected the freedom to sign a "contract" and did not protect the employee's right to work under good working conditions. Employees were often forced to endure harsh treatment. Combined with the unsound factory system at the time, workers' safety and health were not guaranteed at all; especially the cotton spinning factories had particularly poor working conditions, with long hours, low wages, and mostly female and child workers. In 1802, the British Parliament first passed the Health and Morals of Apprentices Act, which limited the working hours of child workers in textile factories, becoming the world's first important occupational safety and health regulation. Britain subsequently promulgated the world's first Factory Act in 1833, which imposed mandatory provisions on workers' labor safety, hygiene, and welfare, becoming a pioneer of the "factory legislation" period.

After the mid-19th century, with the advancement of the Second Industrial Revolution, occupational safety and health legislation gradually developed. Some major capitalist countries followed Britain's lead and joined the ranks of "factory legislation." Occupational safety and health legislation in the United States was first carried out at the state level. At that time, the United States was in the midst of the three-year Civil War (1861–1864, the American Civil War). Factories were in chaos, working conditions were extremely poor, and casualties were frequent. In 1877, Massachusetts promulgated the first Factory Inspection Act in the United States, which greatly promoted the formulation of occupational safety and health regulations in other states.

The 1970s Legislative Wave in OSH

From the early 20th century to the 1970s, capitalism developed into the stage of monopoly capitalism, and industrial production during this period had formed modern industry. The development of science and technology brought rapid changes to production. However, with the application of new technologies, new processes, and new equipment, many new risks and problems were also brought to occupational safety and health. During this period, to adapt to production development, Britain amended the Factory Act four times in 1937, 1948, 1959, and 1961. Especially after the end of World War II, the new technological revolution drove tremendous changes in social economy and production and life, making standardized OSH legislation urgent. European and American countries increasingly felt that only enacting and promulgating single, sporadic occupational safety and health regulations applicable only to a specific scope could no longer meet the needs of industrial development. Occupational safety and health had to be treated as an extremely important issue and a unified, comprehensive, all-encompassing basic safety and health law had to be enacted separately. This legislative preparation process finally came to fruition in the 1970s, setting off a wave of OSH legislation.

In 1970, the United States took the lead in promulgating the Occupational Safety and Health Act. Previously, the United States attempted to implement the Workers' Compensation Act to stimulate enterprise investment and improve working conditions. After that failed, it began to consider formulating an Occupational Safety and Health Act centered on promoting safety and health standards. In January 1968, President Johnson proposed formulating a unified, comprehensive, all-encompassing occupational safety and health plan, including legislative work. By 1970, work-related accidents and occupational hazards were increasingly serious, especially the tragic experience of uranium miners, which shocked the nation. Faced with this grim reality, after intense debate, Congress passed the nationwide unified Occupational Safety and Health Act, which took effect after being signed by President Nixon on December 29, 1970. In this way, the federal government changed the previous situation in which only states formulated safety and health regulations, thereby strengthening leadership over occupational safety and health work in each state.

In 1970, Britain also had many social organizations and institutions, especially trade unions, which questioned whether the existing domestic safety and health regulations were sufficient to protect the safety and health of all workers. Britain subsequently promulgated all provisions of the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act in three batches on October 1, 1974, January 1, 1975, and April 1, 1975. Although several years later than the United States, this regulation was the most comprehensive and rigorous at the time, described as having law within law, strong measures, and detailed provisions, and became a "blueprint" referenced by many countries.

Following the United States and Britain, West Germany promulgated the Occupational Safety Act in 1974, Canada promulgated the Occupational Health and Safety Act in 1978, and Finland promulgated a new Occupational Health Act in 1979. The booming legislative movement in Europe and the US pushed forward the global OSH legislative process. In Asia, Japan promulgated the Industrial Safety and Health Act on June 8, 1972. China's Taiwan region also promulgated the Labor Safety and Health Act on April 16, 1974. In Latin America, Mexico enacted the Safety and Health Act in 1978, and both Bolivia and Venezuela promulgated Safety and Health Acts in 1979. This period can be called the golden age of OSH legislation.

The Reference Value of US OSH Law

Among developed countries in Europe and the US, the US occupational safety and health regulatory system is relatively complete and standardized. The United States enacted the Occupational Safety and Health Act and the Mine Safety and Health Act relatively early, and the related occupational safety and health standards are complete in categories and highly operable. China and the United States are relatively similar in land area, topography, natural climate, industrial categories, population, ethnic groups, and races. Therefore, compared with other developed countries in Europe and the US, the US OSH rule of law construction has strong reference value for China.

Since the promulgation of the Occupational Safety and Health Act in 1970, the United States has established a systematic, comprehensive, and well-functioning occupational safety and health legal system. To ensure the effective implementation of the Occupational Safety and Health Act and the Mine Safety and Health Act, the US Department of Labor established the Occupational Safety and Health Administration and the Mine Safety and Health Administration in 1971 and 1978 respectively. In addition to establishing and improving law enforcement agencies, law enforcement teams, and law enforcement systems, and increasing law enforcement efforts, the United States, in accordance with legal requirements, organizes the formulation and improvement of corresponding occupational safety and health standards and solidly carries out various work around the standards system. In 1971, the United States formulated its first occupational safety and health standard, namely the Workplace Labor Protection Standard. Since then, the United States has successively formulated standards for asbestos dust, lead, benzene, arsenic, and more than 400 toxic substances' maximum threshold values and hearing protection. These standards cover a wide range and relate to public occupational safety and health issues.

At the same time, the United States places great emphasis on the applicability and operability of occupational safety and health standards, gradually improving them during implementation. From the beginning of formulating occupational safety and health standards, it focused on their applicability and operability, staying close to reality so that the vast majority of enterprises could meet or achieve these standards, thereby achieving widespread implementation. Most effective occupational safety and health standards were formulated in the 1980s, such as requiring employers to provide workplaces with hazardous substance concentrations that meet the permissible maximum threshold limit standards during chemical agent production, dangerous goods transportation standards, asbestos dust standards, ethylene oxide standards, benzene concentration standards, formaldehyde (formalin) concentration standards, fire protection standards, hazardous materials disposal and emergency rescue standards, and hazardous nuclear energy sealed disposal standards. Since the 1990s, the United States has also formulated corresponding safety management regulations, space limit permissible standards, construction industry fall protection standards, construction site electrical safety standards, scaffolding standards, occupational exposure to bloodborne pathogenic substances standards, and building lead exposure standards. At the same time, it revised previous formaldehyde standards, methylene chloride standards, personal protective equipment standards, and respirator protection standards.
