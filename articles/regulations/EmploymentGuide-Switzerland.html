Employment Contracts, Social Security, Leave, Termination and EOR Compliance

2026 POLICY UPDATE

AHV/IV/EO Contribution Rates: The 2026 old-age, survivors, and disability insurance (AHV/IV/EO) contribution rate remains 10.6% total, with employers and employees each bearing 5.3% of gross wages. Employers should update payroll parameters accordingly.

BVG Pension Contribution Ceiling: The 2026 mandatory occupational pension (BVG) coordinated salary ceiling and contribution rates are updated. Employers should verify employee age brackets and contribution rates for the mandatory BVG scheme.

Unemployment Insurance Solidarity Contribution: The 2026 unemployment insurance (ALV) contribution rate remains 2.2% total for wages up to CHF 148,200, with employers and employees each bearing 1.1%; a solidarity contribution applies above this threshold. Payroll systems should verify applicable rates.

Hiring employees in Switzerland means companies cannot rely on a single national minimum wage. Switzerland has no statutory national minimum wage, but some cantons and collective agreements set minimum wage requirements. Companies should first confirm the employee's canton of work, applicable collective agreement (GAV), job category, and wage structure, then configure payroll and compliance accordingly.

This guide is intended for HR, finance, legal and overseas business teams of Chinese companies recruiting, signing contracts, running payroll, calculating employer costs, using Employer of Record (EOR) services or handling employee separations in Switzerland. Amounts in this document are expressed in Swiss francs (CHF); specific canton minimum wages, collective agreement rates, occupational pension schemes, and foreign employee right to work still need to be confirmed case by case.

3. Swiss Labor Law and Regulatory Framework

Swiss labor relations are mainly governed by the Swiss Code of Obligations (OR), the Labor Act (ArG), the Federal Act on Old-Age and Survivors' Insurance (AHV), the Federal Act on Occupational Pension (BVG), the Unemployment Insurance Act (AVIG), and collective agreements (GAV). The State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) and cantonal labor authorities are responsible for labor market and working conditions supervision.

Collective agreements may set minimum wages, working hours, overtime, vacation, pension, and termination rules. Companies should confirm the applicable GAV based on industry, company activity, and work location.

The contracting employer bears responsibility for employment contracts, payroll, social security, tax, working environment, leave, and termination. Clients or group managers may provide business input but cannot bypass the Swiss employer to directly discipline or dismiss employees.

4. Recruitment, Job Offer and Onboarding

Recruitment must not discriminate based on gender, age, ethnicity, religion, disability, sexual orientation, or other protected factors. Job advertisements should state the position, work location, wage structure, working hours, and employment type.

Foreign employees must have the right to work consistent with the employer, position, and work location. An employment contract or EOR arrangement cannot replace a work permit.

Switzerland has no national statutory minimum wage, but some cantons (such as Geneva, Neuchâtel, Jura, Ticino) and collective agreements set minimum wage requirements. Companies should verify the applicable minimum wage for the work canton.

10. Local Employees and Foreign Employees

Local and foreign employees in principle enjoy the same labor law protections. Foreign employees must have a work permit consistent with the employer, position, and work location. EOR arrangements do not automatically resolve work permit issues.

Cross-border remote work may trigger tax, social security, permanent establishment, and data protection issues. Employers should assess case by case.

11. Remote Work, Data Privacy and Employee Records

Remote work should be agreed in writing, specifying work location, equipment, expenses, working hours, and data security. The Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP) and GDPR principles apply to employee data processing.

Employee records should be retained per statutory requirements and include contracts, payslips, working time, leave, and termination documents.

Statutory notice periods under the Swiss Code of Obligations are generally: 1 month during the first year of service, 2 months for 2–9 years, and 3 months after 9 years. These may be modified by written agreement or GAV.

Statutory severance pay applies only to employees aged 50 or older with 20 or more years of service, generally 2–8 months' wages depending on age. GAV or contract may provide more favorable terms.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Does Switzerland have a national minimum wage?
No. Switzerland has no national statutory minimum wage, but some cantons and collective agreements set minimum wage requirements. Companies must verify the applicable minimum wage for the work canton.

What is the employer social security rate in Switzerland?
AHV/IV/EO is 5.3% for employer and employee each; ALV is 1.1% each up to CHF 148,200; BVG occupational pension varies by age and scheme.

How many days of annual leave do Swiss employees have?
At least 4 weeks (20 working days) per year; 5 weeks (25 working days) for employees under 20. GAV may provide more.

Can I use an EOR to hire in Switzerland?
Yes, but you must verify the contracting employer, GAV, work permit, and actual management boundaries. EOR does not automatically resolve all compliance issues.
