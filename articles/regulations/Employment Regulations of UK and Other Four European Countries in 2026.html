Employment Regulations of the UK and Four Other European Countries in 2026:
A Comprehensive Interpretation of New European HR Policies

Labor Laws and HR Compliance Guide for the UK, Germany, France, Poland, and Portugal

Source: Overseas Labor Home  |  Date: 2026-04-15

As global supply chains restructure and talent competition intensifies, European countries have successively updated their labor market policies in 2026 to balance local employment protection, skilled talent introduction, and enterprise operational flexibility. For Chinese and multinational enterprises planning or already operating in the UK, Germany, France, Poland, and Portugal, timely understanding of the latest HR policy changes is key to avoiding compliance risks, optimizing employment costs, and improving management efficiency. This article systematically reviews the core labor regulation adjustments in the five countries in 2026 and provides practical HR response recommendations.

1. UK: Comprehensive Reform from "Two-Year Protection" to "Day-One Entitlement"

The UK government passed the Workers' Rights Bill at the end of 2025, to be implemented in stages from April 2026, described as "the largest labor law reform in a generation."

Core Changes:

  • Sick pay from day one: The original "from the fourth day" rule is abolished. Employees are entitled to Statutory Sick Pay (SSP) from the first day of employment if they fall ill. Enterprises must upgrade payroll systems before April 2026.

  • Expanded flexible working rights: All employees (including those on probation) have the right to request flexible working arrangements (such as remote work or compressed hours) from their first day. Employers must respond in writing within two months, and refusals require sufficient business reasons.

  • Zero-hours contract restrictions: "Fire and rehire" (dismissing and rehiring to reduce benefits) is prohibited, with fines of up to GBP 8,000 per employee for violations.

HR Response:

  • Update employment contract templates and clarify the flexible working request process;

  • Establish an automatic sick pay calculation mechanism;

  • Review existing zero-hours/temporary worker arrangements to avoid legal risks.

2. Germany: Strengthening "Fair Integration" and Digital Work Rights

Following the amendment to the Residence Act in 2025, Germany officially implemented the mandatory notification obligation on January 1, 2026, and simultaneously advanced the implementation of the Digital Work Rights Directive.

Core Changes:

  • "Fair Integration" written notice: Employers must provide written notice of free legal counseling services (Faire Integration) in a language the third-country employee can understand on their first day of work, or face fines of up to EUR 30,000.

  • Right to Disconnect: Employees have the right not to respond to work messages after hours, and enterprises may not pressure or assess them for this.

  • AI monitoring restrictions: Using AI systems for emotional analysis, productivity scoring, and other non-transparent monitoring of employees is prohibited.

HR Response:

  • Download the Federal Ministry of Labour multilingual notification template (including Chinese) and include it in the onboarding package;

  • Develop internal "digital communication guidelines" to clarify after-hours response boundaries;

  • Review whether HR SaaS tools comply with data ethics requirements.

3. France: Institutionalization of Remote Work and Skill Retraining Obligations

France continues its "social dialogue" tradition, focusing on improving the remote work framework in 2026 and strengthening enterprises' responsibility for employee skill upgrading.

Core Changes:

  • Mandatory remote work agreement: Where remote work is practiced, a tripartite agreement (employer, employee, union representative) must be signed, clarifying equipment provision, expense reimbursement, health checks, and other terms.

  • Increased Contribution à la Formation (Skill Reinvestment Tax): Enterprises with annual revenue exceeding EUR 1 million must spend 1.6% of total wages on training (up from 1.05%), with shortfalls requiring supplementary payment.

  • Psychological risk assessment: Enterprises must commission a third party every three years to assess psychological stressors in the workplace and formulate improvement plans.

HR Response:

  • Negotiate with unions to develop standardized remote work agreements;

  • Establish employee skill profiles and plan annual training budgets;

  • Introduce EAP (Employee Assistance Program) to meet mental health compliance requirements.

4. Poland: Simplified Blue-Collar Visas but Strengthened Social Security Audits

To alleviate labor shortages in manufacturing and agriculture, Poland optimized the foreign worker introduction process in 2026 while increasing social security compliance supervision.

Core Changes:

  • Electronic seasonal work permits: Seasonal work permits for agriculture, tourism, and other industries can be applied for online, with processing time shortened to 7 working days.

  • Minimum wage increase: From January 2026, the full-time minimum monthly wage rises to PLN 4,300 (approximately EUR 980), with an hourly wage of PLN 28.

  • Normalized social security spot checks: The Labor Inspectorate has the authority to verify foreign employee contracts, payslips, and social security contribution records at any time, with fines of 300% of the arrears for underpayment.

HR Response:

  • Ensure foreign employee contract wages are not lower than the statutory standard;

  • Retain complete employment records (attendance, wages, social security vouchers) for at least 5 years;

  • Use government subsidies to participate in "Polish language + vocational skills" joint training programs.

5. Portugal: Extended Residence for Highly Skilled Talent and Four-Day Workweek Pilot

Portugal continues to implement its "talent attraction strategy," focusing on optimizing high-skilled immigration pathways in 2026 and exploring new working time systems.

Core Changes:

  • Simplified renewal of high-skilled residence permits (Tech Visa): Holders of degrees in ICT, engineering, scientific research, and other fields do not need to re-prove job scarcity when renewing, only providing proof of employment.

  • Expanded four-day workweek pilot: Technology companies in Lisbon, Porto, and other cities can apply to join the government pilot, implementing a 32-hour workweek with unchanged salary.

  • Mandatory gender pay reporting: Enterprises with more than 50 employees must submit annual gender pay gap reports to the Ministry of Labor.

HR Response:

  • Apply for long-term residence for eligible foreign technical employees;

  • Assess business feasibility and apply for the four-day workweek pilot to enhance employer branding;

  • Conduct internal pay audits to ensure gender equality compliance.

Conclusion: Compliance Is Competitiveness

Although the five European countries' HR policies in 2026 have different emphases, the common trend is clear: strengthening workers' basic rights, promoting skill upgrading, regulating digital employment, and cracking down on social security evasion. For enterprises, compliance is no longer just a legal bottom line but a core competitiveness for attracting international talent and building sustainable organizations.

Recommended for companies going global:

  • Establish a localized HR compliance team or entrust professional advisors;

  • Regularly update the employment document library to ensure synchronization with the latest regulations;

  • Incorporate the "S" (Social) dimension of ESG into HR strategy.

Only by actively adapting and systematically planning can enterprises achieve stable and long-term success in Europe's complex and mature labor market.
