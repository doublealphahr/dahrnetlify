EMPLOYMENT GUIDE

McKinsey: Survey of Current HRM Status in America and Europe

Strategic Workforce Planning, Talent Acquisition, Employee Development, Employee Experience, and Reshaping HR Services

 

Published: 2025-10-22

Note: The data in this report comes from McKinsey's 2024 survey of 1,925 companies in Europe and the United States.

1. HR planning lacks strategic focus:

In the United States, only 12% of companies carry out strategic HR planning (3 years or more).

2. Talent acquisition is becoming increasingly complex:

On the one hand, layoffs are increasing and unemployment is rising; on the other hand, the overall recruitment success rate in Europe is only 46%.

3. Employee development remains highly fragmented:

Many organizations still divide employee development into isolated silos. 26% of employees have received no feedback in the past year, and two-thirds of key positions have no succession plan. To prepare employees for future challenges, organizations must link performance management, learning and development, and talent development within a unified strategy.

4. Employee experience is crucial but misaligned:

For employees, job security has become the primary reason they stay (39%), followed by work-life balance (34%) and relationships with colleagues (33%). Nevertheless, many HR departments still focus mainly on optimizing pay and working hours, addressing only part of what employees care about most.

5. Generative AI and shared service centers still have significant room for improving efficiency and effectiveness:

Only 18% of surveyed organizations with more than 1,000 employees use a dedicated SSC in the HR field, and in Europe only 19% of core HR processes are enhanced by generative AI.

1. Strategic HR Planning

According to McKinsey's 2025 "HR Monitor" survey, HR professionals increasingly report that both current and newly hired employees lack the skills needed to meet business needs. According to the survey, 32% of employees do not possess all the skills required to perform their current role. In Europe, 35% of HR professionals list problem-solving as one of the top five future skills, and 30% list data analysis and AI among the top five. At the same time, with the rise of automation and AI, other skills are becoming obsolete. The HR planning we usually use generally takes three forms:

  • Short-term planning (within 1 year);

  • Strategic Workforce Planning (SWP): forecasting workforce needs for the next three to five years based on business strategy and scenario planning.

  • Skills-based SWP: focusing on identifying, developing, and deploying key skills across different time horizons, rather than only focusing on headcount and job roles.

A gap analysis method that forecasts workforce needs based on the skills the company may need in the short and long term is more effective. When conducting skills-based SWP analysis, the following points should be noted:

  • Keep it manageable. Design an executable and easy-to-maintain skills taxonomy, usually covering 20 to 30 core skills across at most 10 to 15 job categories.

  • Build a dynamic, AI-driven skills classification system.

  • Continuously update the skills profile based on business needs, market trends, and emerging technologies.

  • Obtain real-time workforce insights. Use AI to dynamically track employee skills, combining automated analysis with employee input to highlight skill gaps and development needs.

  • Use AI-driven scenario planning and automation to scale SWP. AI can simulate workforce scenarios, predict imbalances, and automate insights, reducing manual workload and lowering adoption barriers.

2. Talent Acquisition

The combined effect of lower offer acceptance rates and high probation turnover rates leads to limited recruitment success. In Europe, on average, only about 46% of candidates who accept an offer remain with the organization after six months. Italy reports the lowest success rate, with only 40% of candidates remaining after six months. In contrast, France performs particularly strongly in offer acceptance rate (65%) and retention rate (59%). The survey shows that the main reasons employees change jobs are as follows:

  1. Pay and additional benefits. 38% of surveyed European employees list pay and additional benefits as the main reason for changing jobs, especially in Germany (42%) and Spain (41%), while the UK places less emphasis on this factor (25%).

  2. Further training and development opportunities. All regions place high importance on training and development opportunities, with an average of 28%. The highest scores are in Spain (33%), Italy, and Poland (both 30%). This factor is least important in the UK (22%) and France (24%).

  3. Flexibility. 27% of employees list flexibility, including flexible working hours and remote or hybrid work arrangements, as the primary factor for changing jobs. Flexibility is particularly important in Poland (31%) and least important in France (24%).

  4. Relationship with managers. 26% of employees list the relationship with managers as a major driver of job changes. This factor is especially important in France (32%) and Germany (30%), while it is least relevant in the UK (22%) and Italy and Poland (both 24%). 25% of employees list work-life balance as one of the main factors prompting job changes. This factor has slightly higher relevance in Germany and Italy (both 26%) and slightly lower relevance in Spain and France (both 23%).

It is recommended to use HR analytics tools or develop internal systems to track candidate preferences and adjust job offers and messaging accordingly. For example, the probation period can be shortened to demonstrate job stability. In addition, generative AI analysis tools can be implemented to gain real-time insights into market trends and deepen collaboration with business departments to clearly understand how role requirements are evolving and assess the urgency of recruitment needs.

3. Employee Development: From Fragmented to Integrated

To maximize the potential of the workforce, HR leaders must approach employee development from three angles:

  • Performance management. Performance management, including feedback and consequence management, helps leaders identify strengths and areas for improvement and address persistent underperformance.

  • Learning and development programs. Adopt targeted training to address individual needs and skill gaps.

  • Talent development. Talent development through strategic approaches such as succession planning can enhance the capabilities of high-performing candidates identified in performance evaluations.

According to the survey, 26% of European employees say they have received no official feedback in the past 12 months. In contrast, HR professionals believe this figure is only 6%. In addition, according to interviews with HR professionals, 56% of European employees say they receive feedback only once or twice a year, which is insufficient for continuous development. Performance management should reflect role-specific goals (such as performance KPIs and technical skills) and expected behaviors to ensure that programs align with business needs and organizational values and culture. In addition to increasing the frequency of performance evaluations to three to four times a year, organizations can establish multi-level feedback systems, including peer-to-peer, upward, and downward feedback. In addition, organizations can participate in "forward-looking feedback" programs that provide employees with future-oriented input, focusing on their upcoming goals and assessing leadership and other behaviors that may complement traditional performance feedback measures. Training is also an important part of employee development and performance management. This is another area where the study found a discrepancy between HR reports and employee experience. European employees report an average of 12 days of training per year, while HR estimates 22 days — a difference of 45%.

4. Employee Experience

Many organizations are reevaluating the employee experience because they recognize its importance in enhancing engagement, reducing absenteeism, and supporting workforce productivity. Signs of dissatisfaction and declining engagement have become more evident: nearly 20% of employees report dissatisfaction with their employer. Despite France's high retention rate, it has the highest dissatisfaction (30%), followed by Italy (27%). Building a strong employee experience can have a significant impact: McKinsey research shows that employees with a positive employee experience are 16 times more engaged and 8 times more likely to stay than employees with a poor experience. What matters most to employees? According to the survey, European employees choose to stay with their current employer for five main reasons:

  1. Job security. Stability is the primary reason employees choose not to leave, with 39% listing it as the main reason they stay. Last year's survey found job security ranked fifth, indicating a significant shift toward stability. Employees place great value on the ability to balance personal and professional responsibilities, with 34% listing work-life balance as the primary reason for staying with their employer.

  2. Relationships with colleagues. A strong sense of belonging and teamwork is crucial to employee satisfaction — 33% of employees list colleague relationships as the primary reason for staying.

  3. Flexibility. Employees place great value on flexibility in when and where they work. 31% of employees list flexibility as the primary reason they stay.

  4. Pay and benefits. Pay and benefits were the main driver last year. Although they are still important to employees, only 28% list them as the primary reason for staying.

From the HR perspective, pay and benefits still rank first among retention factors (41%), followed by work-life balance at 38%. Job security (32%) ranks third, followed by flexibility (30%) and training and development opportunities (25%). The discrepancy between HR departments and employees indicates that HR may not have kept up with changing retention factors. Most notably, HR has consistently significantly underestimated the importance of relationships with colleagues while overestimating the value of pay, benefits, and training. To strengthen employee engagement and motivation, HR should analyze and understand employees' experience in their environment, identify the drivers that truly matter to employees, and design their employee experience accordingly. HR should redesign processes, structures, and procedures based on employees' different needs, align their EX strategy with all stages of the employee lifecycle, and be able to provide proactive, personalized support and continuous engagement. HR can also introduce a buffet-style model that allows employees to personalize benefits (such as mobility, childcare, or wellness) according to their preferences and life stage. Alternatively, they can ensure that all employees have equal access to tools, support, and development opportunities, regardless of where they work, to improve the underlying infrastructure of their work.

5. Reshaping HR Services

SSC and AI. According to McKinsey's analysis, high-performing organizations are using automation and generative AI to improve efficiency, reducing the ratio from 1 HR person per 70 employees to 1 per 200 employees. In addition, some organizations are now expanding shared service centers (SSCs) or piloting technology-driven models that integrate generative AI and automation directly into HR service delivery, potentially skipping the traditional SSC structure. At the same time, generative AI offers HR an opportunity to elevate itself from a cost-centered function to a strategic partner — using data-driven insights to drive transformation and shape better business decisions.

SSCs remain a key enabler of efficiency, but as organizations adopt generative AI, their role must evolve. Organizations considering launching an SSC should carefully evaluate whether this is still the right path, because generative AI-driven automation and self-service alone can provide a more agile and cost-effective alternative. It is recommended to reevaluate the future role of the SSC to strike the right balance between centralized service delivery and generative AI-driven automation, retaining the SSC as the primary tool for handling complex HR cases and employee support. The current level of AI application in HR shows clear regional differences: in the United States, AI has been applied to 35% of core HR processes, while in Europe it is only 19%. In both regions, about 30% of core HR processes are in the pilot stage, indicating that application is growing but still selective.

HR professionals estimate that by adopting generative AI, about one-third of all HR work can be automated. Enterprises should start with high-impact use cases while developing a broader generative AI strategy in parallel. To truly scale transformation, organizations should ultimately aim to have a broader generative AI portfolio containing 15 to 20 priority HR use cases or more — not just a few pilot projects. Generative AI is not just an efficiency lever for HR; it also provides an opportunity to rethink HR processes and improve service delivery. Organizations should act immediately by implementing obvious, high-impact use cases (such as content creation in recruitment, personalized learning recommendations, or generative AI-supported workforce planning and forecasting) while developing a broader generative AI strategy in parallel to ensure long-term scalability, security, and consistency.
